# Tuning the AirBand constants

## Status of the constants

Every threshold, time constant and range in the DSP of v1.3.2 was carried over from convention and none was swept or ablated against reference material; the source comments in VocalGate.h, VocalCompressor.h, VocalLimiter.h and AirBand.cpp said so. This document records the behavior of the stages as measured on the v1.3.2 release, which is the baseline of every comparison below, what the primary sources say about the corresponding quantities in comparable designs, the order in which the constants were revisited, and the results of that work in the final section. The probes in docs/tuning reproduce the measurements. StageCurvesProbe.cpp prints static and dynamic curves for each stage, LowLevelResponseProbe.cpp prints the low-level frequency response of the air stages, VocalLevelsProbe.cpp prints the level distributions a real recording presents to each stage, VocalRenderProbe.cpp renders a recording through the DSP and measures what each setting adds, SibilanceProbe.cpp writes per-frame band envelopes and the output of the sibilance detector for sibilance_scores.py to score against labels taken from a spectrogram, and LevelTrackerProbe.cpp compares the level tracker with the true percentiles of a recording. The first two are built by compiling against libairband_core.a and the Harness, TestSignals and TestMetrics objects of the AirBand_Tests target and the others against libairband_core.a alone; VocalRenderProbe compiles the AirBand sources in, so that the released code and a variant of a constant can be rendered side by side. The outputs for v1.3.2 carry that suffix and the outputs for the current code sit beside them.

## The air path and the dry path are not time aligned

AirBand::process passes the side path through a two-times oversampler built on a polyphase IIR half-band filter, whose reported latency is 3.137 samples at 44.1 kHz, while AirBandDSP sums that path with the dry signal without delaying the dry signal. The sum is therefore a comb filter. With Mid Air at 15 dB and blend at its 20 percent default, the response at low level is +4.7 dB at 4 kHz, -6.7 dB at 7 kHz, -16.6 dB at 8 kHz, +5.4 dB at 12 kHz and -8.5 dB at 16 kHz, and High Air at 15 dB gives +2.2 dB at 6 kHz, -8.0 dB at 10 kHz and +5.3 dB at 14 kHz. A delay of 3.137 samples is a half period at 7.03 kHz, and the phase lead of the 3 kHz high-pass moves the cancellation to about 8 kHz, which agrees with the measurement. Because this ripple exceeds the effect of any level constant, thresholds cannot be judged by ear until the two paths are aligned.

## Ranges of the air controls

The Mid Air and High Air controls are labelled in decibels, but the boost a listener obtains depends on the blend. A knob value of 15 dB yields 3.2 dB at 5 kHz at 20 percent blend and 13.4 dB at 100 percent blend; a knob value of 10 dB yields 1.2 dB at 20 percent. The Dolby A encoder that the effect is modelled on has four bands at 80 Hz low-pass, 80 Hz to 3 kHz, 3 kHz high-pass and 9 kHz high-pass with 12 dB per octave slopes, a compander threshold of -40 dB, a ratio of 2:1 for a gain change of 10 dB, and about 10 dB of noise reduction rising to 15 dB at 15 kHz because the two high-pass contributions stack (Wikipedia, Dolby noise-reduction system). A search summary also gave 5 dB for the 9 kHz band, which the fetched page does not state.

## The air law threshold

The boost collapses as the band envelope approaches a threshold of -6 dBFS, with a quadratic knee in linear amplitude. At low level the boost is within 0.2 dB of its maximum up to a band level of -54 dBFS, falls to about half at -20 dBFS and is gone at -6 dBFS. The Dolby A threshold is stated relative to an unspecified reference level; if that level is -18 dBFS the transition lies between about -58 and -38 dBFS, which is 20 to 30 dB below the AirBand knee. Whether ordinary vocal high-band content sits on the plateau of the AirBand law, where the boost is static, depends on the distribution of band levels in real vocals and is measured in the results section below.

## Gate

The gate threshold is a fixed -40 dBFS on a near-peak envelope. At full amount a tone is reduced by 0.3 dB at -40 dBFS, 4.0 dB at -45, 7.8 dB at -50 and by the 18 dB cap from -64 dBFS downward. The 200 ms release is a property of the level detector, so after a phrase at -20 dBFS ends into a tail at -50 dBFS no attenuation is applied for about 400 ms and the full reduction arrives after about 900 ms. A 60 Hz rumble at -45 dBFS reduces the attenuation applied to -50 dBFS voice content from 7.8 dB to 1.8 dB, because the detector has no side-chain filter. Mix-engineering guidance for vocal gates gives attack times of 1 to 3 ms, hold times of 20 to 100 ms and release times of 50 to 120 ms (musicguymixing.com), and FabFilter Pro-G exposes threshold, ratio and range as its primary controls. Side-chain filters act only on the detection signal (Audio Masterclass).

## Compressor, de-esser and limiter

The compressor has a hard knee at -18 dBFS on a near-peak follower, a ratio of up to 4:1 and a flat makeup gain of 6 dB at full amount that applies to all material below threshold, so a quiet signal gains 6.0 dB and a signal at 0 dBFS loses 6.9 dB. Giannoulis, Massberg and Reiss recommend feed-forward designs whose detector sits after the gain computer in the log domain and whose knee width is variable; the full text was not retrievable and only its abstract-level description was read. A page of vocal compression settings (musicguymixing.com) reports 5 to 15 dB of gain reduction on the loudest peaks for a lead vocal at 8:1, says that a softer knee gives more transparent compression, and advises setting the makeup gain so that the level matches the vocal before compression; a search summary also gave 3 to 6 dB of reduction on the loudest notes, which the fetched page does not state. The sibilance ratio of the de-esser, the level of a 6.5 kHz band-pass divided by the level of the 9 kHz high-pass band, measures 0.57 for white noise, 2.07 for noise between 3 and 6 kHz, 1.39 for noise between 5 and 8 kHz and 0.34 for noise between 10 and 16 kHz, and the code clamps it at 1, so sibilant content saturates it. The control reduces only the boost of the 9 kHz band, while the 3 kHz band, which covers male sibilance at 3 to 6 kHz and female sibilance at 5 to 8 kHz, has no de-esser. The limiter's 5 ms lookahead and 60 ms release are conventional and no evidence argues for changing them.

## Order of work

The air path is aligned first, with the golden vectors re-recorded because the change alters the sound. The distribution of band levels is then measured on real vocal material, the air knees are chosen from those distributions and checked with level-matched renders, and the gate is restructured so that the detector is fast, the gain has separate attack, hold and release, the threshold is a parameter and the detector carries a high-pass. The sibilance measure is chosen by scoring candidates against frame labels taken from a spectrogram of the recording. The compressor receives a soft knee and a threshold and makeup placed relative to a reference level, and the same kind of reference is given to the air knees, so that every level constant that depends on how hot the material was tracked follows the material.

## Results

The measurements in this section were taken on a 155 second mono 44.1 kHz phone voice memo of a vocal performance (AAC at 61 kb/s, so its content ends near 10.7 kHz and the 9 kHz band holds only 9 to 10.7 kHz). The recording is a single take at a single tracking level, with a broadband envelope median of -20.4 dBFS and a 99th percentile of -11.7 dBFS, and it is not part of the repository. Through AirBand's own filters and envelope detector the 3 kHz band has a median level of -38.6 dBFS, a 90th percentile of -31.1 and a 99th percentile of -25.2, and the 9 kHz band has a median of -50.6, a 90th percentile of -43.0 and a 99th percentile of -33.3. The boost law with its knee at -6 dBFS applied between 94 and 99 percent of the maximum boost at every percentile of both bands, so on this material it behaved as a static shelf.

The air path is aligned with the dry path. The air bands use a linear-phase FIR half-band oversampler, whose impulse response is exactly symmetric and whose latency of 49 samples is a whole number, and the dry signal is delayed by the same 49 samples before the sum, so the plugin reports 270 samples of latency at 44.1 kHz instead of 221. At low level the response of Mid Air at 15 dB and 20 percent blend is now a smooth shelf of -1.2 dB at 2 kHz, +1.5 dB at 3 kHz, +4.6 dB at 5 kHz and +5.6 dB above 12 kHz, and High Air behaves alike above 9 kHz. The dip of 1.2 to 1.4 dB just below each band's corner is the phase lead of the high-pass in a parallel sum and is the largest cut at any frequency. At 100 percent blend the High Air band cuts by 5 dB at 4 kHz for the same reason. On the recording, the released v1.3.2 with both Air knobs at 9 dB, 40 percent blend and De-Ess at 50 percent lowered the 9 kHz band by 3.1, 3.6 and 4.3 dB in the quietest, middle and loudest thirds of the active frames, and the aligned path raises it.

The air knees are placed relative to a reference level, the 90th percentile of each band's own envelope. The design references are -31 dBFS for the 3 kHz band and -43 dBFS for the 9 kHz band, the values measured on the recording, and each knee lies 5 dB above its reference, at -26 and -38 dBFS, which is 12 dB above each band's median level, so that the boost collapses on the loudest percent of frames and about half of it remains at the median. With both Air knobs at 9 dB, 40 percent blend, De-Ess at 50 percent and the references held at their design values, the lift of the 9 kHz band is 3.38, 2.72 and 1.64 dB in the quietest, middle and loudest thirds of the active frames and that of the 3 kHz band is 1.57, 1.20 and 0.50 dB. A knee at -6 dBFS on both bands, as in v1.3.2, gives 4.98, 4.92 and 4.51 dB and 2.46, 2.45 and 2.06 dB, a spread between the quietest and loudest thirds of 0.47 and 0.40 dB, so that the boost behaves as a static shelf, and knees of -20 and -32 dBFS give 4.21, 3.84 and 2.96 dB and 2.02, 1.81 and 1.13 dB. Raising both knobs to 14 dB with the chosen knees gives 6.62, 5.49 and 3.41 dB at 9 kHz and 3.89, 3.04 and 1.31 dB at 3 kHz.

The reference follows the level of the material while Auto Level is on. A LevelTracker fed by each band's envelope adds the level to a histogram of 1 dB bins every 10 ms, fades the histogram with a 20 s time constant and reads the level that 10 percent of the faded observations exceed. A pseudo-observation of one second at the design level covers the start, levels under -90 dBFS are not counted, and the estimate is confined to 18 dB around the design level. The compressor takes its reference from the same tracker, fed by its follower. Against the true 90th percentile of the whole recording, the tracked reference has a mean absolute error after the first 20 s of 1.2 dB for the 3 kHz band, 0.8 dB for the 9 kHz band and 0.8 to 0.9 dB for the compressor follower, and the error is the same for copies of the recording 12 dB quieter and 12 dB louder. During the first 20 s the estimate follows the recent level, which on this recording lies up to 5.6 dB under the long-term percentile. Rendered through the DSP at the settings above with tracking on, the lift of the 9 kHz band in the three thirds is 3.24, 2.53 and 1.71 dB for the recording 12 dB quieter, 3.30, 2.53 and 1.60 dB at its own level and 3.31, 2.50 and 1.60 dB for the recording 6 dB louder, and that of the 3 kHz band is 1.41, 1.01 and 0.49 dB, 1.48, 1.06 and 0.49 dB and 1.49, 1.07 and 0.50 dB. With the references held the same three renderings give 4.62, 4.46 and 4.02 dB, 3.38, 2.72 and 1.64 dB and 2.15, 1.27 and 0.45 dB at 9 kHz, so that a quiet recording meets a nearly static shelf and a hot one a boost that is mostly collapsed. On program-like noise bursts spread over 25 dB, the lift of the loudest third differs by 0.06 dB between the signal and a copy 12 dB quieter with tracking on and by 5.7 dB with the references held, and the loudest tenth of the compressor's frames changes by -1.11 and -1.05 dB with tracking on and by -7.95 and +0.90 dB with the references held.

The sibilance measure of the released De-Ess, the level of a 6.5 kHz band-pass divided by the level of the 9 kHz band and clamped at 1, separates the recording's sibilant frames from its voiced frames with an area under the ROC curve of 0.056, which is to say that it reads higher on voiced frames (median 3.6) than on sibilant ones (median 1.9), because the band above 9 kHz holds so little of the recording that the ratio exceeds the clamp on every sibilant frame (10th percentile 1.55) and on more than half of the voiced frames. The labels came from a spectrogram of the recording: of 6471 active frames of 20 ms, 280 have more power between 4 and 9 kHz than between 100 Hz and 1 kHz and are called sibilant, and 5714 have 15 dB less and are called voiced; on a 10 s excerpt of the spectrogram the bright 5 to 9 kHz bursts coincide with the frames so labelled. Dividing the level of a band-pass by the level of the whole signal instead gives areas of 0.985 to 0.991 for the candidates scored (sibilance_scores.out). The measure adopted is the level of a fourth-order band-pass at 6 kHz (Q 1.5 per section) over the level of the whole signal, both followed with a 1 ms attack and 50 ms release, mapped through a smooth ramp from 0.08 to 0.35. Its ratio has a median of 0.025 and a 99th percentile of 0.128 over voiced frames and a median of 0.39 and a 10th percentile of 0.11 over sibilant frames, and the detector's output averages 0.756 on sibilant frames (75 percent above 0.5) and 0.008 on voiced frames (1.2 percent above 0.2), with an area of 0.990. A single detector runs per channel and serves both bands, and each band scales its boost by one minus De-Ess times the sibilance, so that the boost is withdrawn from sibilants and never becomes a cut; with both Air knobs at 0 dB De-Ess has nothing to scale. At those settings the released code altered the 9 to 10.7 kHz band of the recording by +0.36 dB because De-Ess acted on a band whose boost was zero, and the present code leaves it unchanged. With both Air knobs at 9 dB and 40 percent blend, De-Ess at 0, 50 and 100 percent changes the level of the 3 to 9 kHz band on sibilant frames by +0.96, +0.66 and +0.37 dB and that of the 9 to 10.7 kHz band by +1.52, +1.10 and +0.65 dB, while on voiced frames the changes stay at +1.19 and +3.05 dB; the released De-Ess moved the 9 to 10.7 kHz band by 5.5 dB on sibilant frames and by 3.6 dB on voiced ones, a large change on both classes. On band-limited noise at -66 dBFS with both Air knobs at 15 dB, the boost of 5 to 8 kHz noise falls from 5.19 dB to 2.70 dB and to 0.00 dB as De-Ess rises from 0 to 50 and 100 percent, that of 10 to 16 kHz noise from 8.01 dB to 7.63 dB and 7.23 dB, and noise below 1.5 kHz is unchanged. Flat noise reads as fully sibilant, as it has as much level in the band as a fricative of its width, a lone tone at 10 kHz reads 0.37 and one at 12 kHz 0.004.

The compressor has a 6 dB quadratic soft knee centred on its threshold, a ratio from 1:1 to 4:1, a threshold 3 dB below the reference level and a makeup gain equal to the reduction applied at the reference level. The design reference is -18.6 dBFS, the 90th percentile of the follower on the recording (5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 95th and 99th percentiles -64.6, -31.1, -24.2, -20.8, -17.6 and -15.8 dBFS), so the threshold starts at -21.6 dBFS and the reference level itself passes at unity gain: material under the knee is raised by the makeup, +1.29, +1.80 and +2.25 dB at amounts of 25, 50 and 100 percent, and a tone at 0 dBFS is lowered by 7.65, 10.70 and 13.38 dB, where v1.3.2 raised everything under its -18 dBFS threshold by a flat 6.0 dB and lowered a tone at 0 dBFS by 6.9 dB. On the recording at an amount of 100 percent the 50 ms frame levels in dBFS, at the 10th, 50th, 90th and 99th percentiles and the maximum, are -45.6, -27.8, -22.7, -20.1 and -17.9 at the input, -43.3, -22.0, -17.0, -15.3 and -13.8 for v1.3.2 and -45.1, -26.4, -22.5, -21.1 and -19.6 now; the spread between the median and the 99th percentile is 7.7 dB at the input, 6.7 dB for v1.3.2 and 5.3 dB now, and the median rises by 1.4 dB where it rose by 5.8 dB. The threshold of v1.3.2 lay at the 93rd percentile of the follower, so that 6.8 percent of the samples were above it and a ratio of 4:1 reduced the largest follower value by 3.4 dB; the present threshold lies at the 68th percentile, with 32 percent of the samples above it and a largest reduction of 6.1 dB before the knee is taken into account.

The gate is restructured. The detector follows the signal with a 1 ms attack and a 20 ms release after a 100 Hz second-order high-pass that touches only the detection path, the expander law maps the level to a reduction in dB, and the reduction is smoothed with a 2 ms opening, a 40 ms hold and a 100 ms release; all channels share the loudest channel's level and one gain. The threshold is the Gate Threshold parameter, from -70 to -20 dBFS with a default of -40 dBFS. Behind a one second phrase at -20 dBFS the attenuation of a -50 dBFS tail is now 3.0 dB at 100 ms, 6.3 dB at 200 ms and 8.05 dB once settled, where it was zero for 400 ms and settled after 900 ms, and a 60 Hz rumble 5 dB above the voice lessens the attenuation of the voice by 2.2 dB instead of 6.0 dB. On the whole recording at a gate amount of 60 percent, the released gate lowers the frames below -60 dBFS by 0.95 dB on average, those between -60 and -50 dBFS by 0.18 dB and those between -50 and -45 dBFS by 0.09 dB, because the 200 ms detector release rarely lets the envelope fall under -40 dBFS between phrases. The new gate at a threshold of -40 dBFS lowers the same frames by 6.03, 2.78 and 1.02 dB, the frames between -45 and -40 dBFS by 0.26 dB, those between -40 and -35 dBFS by 0.13 dB and those above -35 dBFS by at most 0.01 dB. At a threshold of -34 dBFS the figures are 7.28, 4.42, 3.00, 0.98, 0.36 and 0.03 dB.

The Air knobs are the boost at 100 percent blend, and what a setting adds at low level is the sum of the dry path and the blended Mid and High Air paths, whose high-passes lead in phase. AirBandDSP::lowLevelGainDb computes that sum from the settings and the editor shows it at 5 kHz and 12 kHz beside the knobs; for 9 dB on both bands at 40 percent blend it reads 3.3 dB and 6.8 dB. The function agrees with the measured response of the DSP within 0.065 dB at eight frequencies from 500 Hz to 16 kHz for four combinations of the three controls. The Auto Level control is a host parameter, on by default, that switches the level tracking of the air knees and the compressor threshold; with it off the references are held at their design values and the stages follow static laws.

The checks in Tests cover the alignment (Air 4, and Linear 3 verifies at three sample rates that the reported latency is the true delay), the gate ballistics, threshold, detector filter and stereo link (Dynamics 4 to 7), the compressor's static law (Dynamics 1 and Tracking 4), the tracker, the invariance of the air lift and of the compressor to the level of the material, the sibilance measure and the low-level gain function (Tracking 1 to 6), De-Ess (Air 3, which measures the boost of the 9 kHz band's own content with and without sibilant energy and that full De-Ess leaves the level at the dry level and never below it) and the new host parameter. The static-law checks run with level tracking off. The golden vectors were re-recorded and a thirteenth, with tracking off, was added. The result is 154 passing checks in AirBand_Tests and 13 in AirBand_HostTests on macOS arm64, bit-identical block partitioning at block size 1 on arm64 and on x86_64 under Rosetta, golden drift on x86_64 of at most 6.0e-5 dB RMS and 5.8e-7 relative peak against tolerances of 5e-4 dB and 1e-6, no RealtimeSanitizer finding and no static realtime warning, and a plugin that builds as VST3, AU and Standalone. The current vectors have not been measured on Windows. An offscreen rendering of the editor shows the ninth knob, the Auto Level toggle and the readout fitting in an 855 by 244 pixel window. Rendering the recording takes 0.5 s more per 155 s of mono audio than v1.3.2 (2.06 s against 1.56 s, report analysis included).

Several findings remain open. The design references, the 5 dB knee offset, the 3 dB compressor offset, the 6 dB knee width, the sibilance ramp and the tracker's percentile, memory and window were derived from one recording, a phone memo with a band-limited spectrum tracked at a single level, or carried over from convention, and none was auditioned by ear. The tracker state depends on what has played, so after a jump of the transport the references stand where the earlier material left them and a render started in the middle of a song starts from the design values; Auto Level off removes this dependence. Flat noise and a lone tone between 5 and 10 kHz read as partly or fully sibilant. The compressor smooths the rectified signal in the linear domain before the conversion to dB, where the feed-forward designs recommended in the literature detect in the log domain. Level-matched renders of the recording for auditioning the variants are written to local/renders, which is excluded from the repository because the recording is private.
